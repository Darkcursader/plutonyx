# One-page lesson guide — PROJECT JEZERO in the classroom

**Ages:** 7–12 · **Time:** one lesson (40–50 minutes) · **Needs:** any laptop, Chromebook or tablet with a browser; one device per pair works well · **Link:** https://darkcursader.github.io/plutonyx/

## What students will learn

By the end, students can explain in their own words:

1. **Power is a budget.** Air, food, shielding and science all draw on the same limited supply, so choosing one means giving up some of another.
2. **Space weather is real and sudden.** The Sun can send a burst of radiation that reaches Mars in well under an hour.
3. **Mars is far away.** A radio message takes many minutes each way, so a crew must make urgent decisions alone.
4. **Forecasts are best guesses.** Preparing for a storm that does not come costs something; not preparing for one that does costs more.

## Set-up (5 minutes)

1. Open the link and choose **Start mission → Classroom**. Classroom mode has no timers, gives hints, and pauses at four points with a question for the class.
2. To have every pair fly **the same mission** (same dust, same breakdowns): one pair finishes a mission, reads out the **mission code** from the debrief, and the others enter it under **Replay history → Have a mission code?**
3. Tell students: *"You are the Commander. Nobody dies in this game, but you can lose things you care about. Every number about the Sun and radiation is real."*

## During play (25–30 minutes)

Let pairs talk through each choice. The game pauses for discussion after Sols 3, 6, 8 and 10:

| After | Question on screen | What to listen for |
|---|---|---|
| Sol 3 | Which machine did your team turn down first? What did it cost you later? | Delayed consequences; "because you…" messages |
| Sol 6 | The forecast said a solar cloud was coming. Did you charge the shield? What did you give up? | Trade-offs under uncertainty |
| Sol 8 | Earth could not help during the storm. Why not? How long did a message take? | Light-time delay; Earth could not see that side of the Sun |
| Sol 10 | If you were Dil, would you have stayed with the core or driven home? Is there a right answer? | No clean answer; respectful disagreement |

Encourage students to tap the green **REAL DATA** chips. Each opens the real numbers for that day and where they come from.

## After play (10 minutes)

- Read the **"It really happened"** reveal together. Sol 8 was 20 May 2024. Ask: *does knowing it was real change how you feel about your choices?*
- Compare endings across the room. Different choices lead to seven different endings; ask two pairs with different endings to explain what they protected.
- Open **Science log** from the menu to see which NASA data the game uses and what the game simplified.

## Quick extensions

- **Maths:** A radio signal travels at 300,000 km per second. On 20 May 2024 Mars was 284.7 million km from Earth. How many minutes did a message take? (About 15.8.)
- **Science:** Earth has a magnetic field that steers many solar particles away. Mars has no global magnetic field today. What difference does that make on the ground?
- **Design:** Draw a shelter for a solar storm using only things already at the outpost. (Real idea: water and rock make good shields.)
- **Food:** The Canadian Space Agency's toolkit *Food production on the Moon and in remote areas* has classroom activities on growing food in space: https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/youth-educators/toolkits/food-production/

## Good to know

- No accounts, no advertising, no personal data. Progress (endings found, settings) is stored only on the device.
- Settings include larger text, read-aloud, reduced motion and colour-blind patterns.
- The game is honest about what it changed: dose limits are scaled for a short mission, plants grow faster than in real life, and the powered storm shield is future technology. Radiation never makes a character sick; it fills a badge.
